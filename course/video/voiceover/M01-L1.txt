The Idea Form
(Read at about 130 words a minute. UK English.)

Isla's aunt, Mara Quill, breeds dogs and holds a dog breeding licence from her local council. Every time the licence comes up for renewal, Mara spends weeks hunting through folders for paperwork. Isla wanted to turn that headache into an app idea. She wrote two full pages of features: reminders, charts, a shop, a chat room. When she showed it to her aunt, Mara could not tell what the app was actually for. Then Isla learned how the factory starts every idea. Not with features, but with three short answers: what it is, who pays, and what hurts.

Every idea begins at Stage 0, called Idea, in a room called Reception. Reception is free: nothing here costs a single build credit. By the end of Stage 0, the idea will have five things. A card, which is its record. A one-line brief. A kill check, which asks whether the idea should stop now. A set of 20 codes that describe how the app handles businesses, people and data. And a data role, which says who is responsible for the personal data. This lesson covers the first part: the Idea Form, and the doors ideas come through.

Ideas can arrive through four doors. The first is the Idea Form, filled in by a person with an idea. The second is called the flip farm: ideas that arrive already attached to an existing template, so they start with a ready-made shape. The third is the AI door, for ideas suggested by an AI tool. The fourth is the client door, for ideas sent in by outside customers, and it is not built yet. The door an idea came through is recorded on its record, so anyone can later see where it started.

Now the form itself. It opens with a short name and a vision. The vision is one line that says what the app is and what it does, with no list of features. Isla rewrote hers: Litterline, a licence and records hub for dog breeders. That is the whole vision. It is short on purpose. A short line can be checked, argued with and changed. Two pages of features cannot. If you cannot say what your app is in one line, the idea is not ready yet, and that is useful to know before any work starts.

Next, the form asks who pays. This means the person or business whose money keeps the app running, not just who uses it. For Litterline, breeders pay. But there is a second answer: dog clubs can resell it under their own name. A reseller is a business that sells the app to its own members with its own name and colours on it. In our example, the fictional club Northvale Canine Society resells Litterline to its breeders. Naming the payer early matters, because a later gate, Lock, will not let an app move on without one.

The third question is pain. The pain is the problem the app removes, written the way the customer would say it. For Litterline, the pain is that licence renewals mean weeks of paper hunting. Notice that this is not a feature. It does not say reminders or charts. It names something that hurts, and that a breeder would recognise straight away. Good pain lines are plain and specific. A vague pain, such as making life easier, cannot be tested. A specific pain can be checked against real customers in Stage 1, Research.

When the form is sent, the factory engine does five things at once. The engine is the script that moves apps along the road. It writes the idea's card. It creates the app's task at Stage 0, or on hold if the data is sensitive. It adds a row to the list of ideas. It suggests a verdict, which is pass, hold or kill. And it suggests a data role. It also checks whether the factory has seen this idea before, and flags it if so. If a form is ever lost, a safety net writes the card on the next check.

The card is the single home of the idea's classification. It is never overwritten. If something changes, a new version is saved beside it, marked v2, then v3, so the history is always there. There is also an optional extra: a one-screen sketch of the idea, using made-up data and no tables, so a person can picture it. It costs nothing. Isla now has a one-line vision, a named payer and a clear pain, instead of two pages of features. Next, the form asks its biggest question: the 20 codes. First, a recap.
