Choosing Kits And Bricks
(Read at about 130 words a minute. UK English.)

Sam was helping to plan Stayproof, our sample inspection pack for boarding kennels. He wanted the first version to do everything a kennel could ever need: daily logs, staff rotas, feeding charts, a shop, reviews, a chat room. Every day the list grew longer, and nothing on it could be built or tested, because nothing was finished. Then someone asked him a different question. What is the smallest app a first customer would pay for and actually use? Everything else could wait on a roadmap. That question is the heart of Stage 3, called Features.

Stage 3 is where the bricks and kits from Stage 2 become a real feature list. It sits in the Research room and it is free. The work is written into a build pack, a set of short notes kept in the app's one document organised by stage, before anything is built. The pack answers five things. Who sees and does what. Which words, labels and colours are data. How payment works. Which tenancy level the app has, using the TEN (0-6) code. And the launch-minimum scope. We will take them one at a time.

The first note is a who-sees-and-does-what map. It has one line for each role and each action. A role is a type of person using the app, such as owner, staff member or customer. A line might say: kennel staff can add a daily log, and only for their own kennel. Each line also names where the check happens on the server side, which means on the computer that holds the data, not just on the screen. A rule that lives only on the screen can be dodged. A rule on the server cannot.

The second note is content as data. Every word, label, colour and category the app shows is listed and stored as rows in a table, never typed into the code. Why bother? Because a reseller app like Litterline wears different brand names. When Northvale Canine Society offers the app to its breeders, the name, colours and wording change, but the layout stays the same. If the words are rows, that swap is an edit to a few rows. If the words are baked into the code, every change becomes a paid building turn.

The third and fourth notes are about money and businesses. The payment route says who takes the money, who is the merchant of record, meaning the business named on the customer's receipt, and how VAT is handled. The PAY (0-2) code helps decide this. The tenancy level uses TEN (0-6), which counts how many businesses share the app and who sits above them. Stayproof is TEN 2: several kennels side by side, none above another. Litterline is TEN 3, because one reseller club sits above its breeders. A higher level needs more walls.

The fifth note is the launch-minimum scope. It is the fewest tables and screens that let a first customer pay and use the app. Everything else goes on the roadmap, which is the list of things to add later. Alongside it sits an in and out list, so everyone can see what was left out on purpose. One checklist rule keeps the scope honest: the first build must fix the three complaints customers mentioned most in research, and include at least one thing no rival has. Small, but already better than what is out there.

Before anything is built, the app gets a clickable mockup. A mockup is a pretend version of the screens you can tap through, with no real data behind it. It is designed phone first, then checked at laptop width, and it follows the house style rules. The mockup also has to use everything in the research: the tables, the rivals, the price range. A mockup made only from colours and calm layouts tends to look nice and say nothing. The person who decides at Lock clicks through it at both widths and says yes.

One more thing goes into Stage 3: a journey map. A journey is one path a person takes through the app, such as signing up, checking an email on another device, signing in, resetting a password or signing out. Each journey is listed and questioned at design time. What does a signed-out visitor see on each page? Does it still work in a new tab? Thinking this through now is free. Finding a broken journey after a paid build is not. Next lesson covers the duties list and the gate that freezes all this. First, a recap.
