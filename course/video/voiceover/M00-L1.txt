What An App Is Made Of
(Read at about 130 words a minute. UK English.)

Ava is thirteen and helps on Saturdays at The Tidy Paw, a dog grooming salon run by Juno Platt. Juno keeps her bookings on paper cards, and every week a few clients simply do not turn up. Ava wanted to build Juno an app that would fix this. She opened an app builder, stared at the blank page, and stopped. She did not know where to begin. Then she noticed something that changed how she saw it: every app, however big, is made of the same four things. Screens, tables, rules and people. Name those four first, and the blank page stops being blank.

Start with screens. A screen is one page of an app that a person looks at and touches, such as a calendar, a form or a list. For Juno's salon, one screen might show today's bookings. Another might let a client pick a time. A screen does not store anything on its own. It only shows information and collects it. That matters, because a lot of people think the screens are the app. They are only the front window. Behind every screen sits the part that remembers things, and that is where we go next.

Behind the screens sit tables. A table is where an app stores its data, laid out in rows and columns, rather like a spreadsheet. Each row is one thing, such as one booking. Each column is one fact about it, such as the date, the time or the dog's name. Juno's salon would need a table of bookings, a table of clients and a table of free time slots. The plan of all the tables an app needs is called a schema. Get the tables right and the screens are easy to change later. Get them wrong and everything wobbles.

Next come rules. A rule decides who may see something and who may change it. Juno should see all her own clients. A client should see only their own bookings, never anyone else's. If a second salon used the same app, neither salon should ever see the other's data. In this course, a rule that keeps one group's data apart from another's is called a wall. When a wall fails and someone sees data that is not theirs, that is called a leak. A leak is the most serious mistake an app can make, so the factory tests for leaks again and again.

The fourth thing is people. Every app has different kinds of people using it, and each kind needs different screens and different rules. There is the owner who runs the app. There are the businesses that use it, like Juno's salon. There are those businesses' customers, like the dog owners who book a trim. And there is the public: strangers who have not signed in at all. These ideas come back in the four groups of codes you will meet in Stage 0. Knowing exactly who signs in, and who does not, shapes every table and every rule.

So why does this matter? Most apps today are made by hand. Someone starts from a blank page, invents the screens, the tables and the rules from nothing, and makes hundreds of choices along the way. The next app starts from a blank page again. The same mistakes get made twice, and the same safety checks get forgotten twice. Nothing learned on one app is carried to the next. That can work for a single app. It does not work if you want many apps that are all safe, all tested and all built the same careful way.

An app factory takes a different route. Every idea travels the same road, in the same order, with the same checklist at each step. Instead of inventing parts from nothing, the factory reuses tested parts called bricks. There are 101 bricks, kept on 8 shelves, and they are grouped into 9 ready-made bundles called kits, numbered K00-K08. Kit K00 is the floor that every app gets: the basics, the walls between businesses, and the legal and consent parts. When a problem is found on one app, the fix becomes a checklist line, so the next app does not repeat it.

Let us put it together. Ava now has a plan before she builds anything. For Juno's salon she can list the screens: today's bookings and a booking page. She can list the tables: bookings, clients and time slots. She can write the rules: each client sees only their own bookings, and each salon sees only its own clients. And she can name the people: Juno, her clients and the public. That list is the start of every app the factory makes. Next you will see the road an idea travels, its rooms and its gates.
