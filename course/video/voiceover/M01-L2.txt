The 20 Codes
(Read at about 130 words a minute. UK English.)

Rory is twelve and was helping his sister fill in the Idea Form for Litterline. Everything went smoothly until he reached a list of 20 short questions with odd capital-letter names like TEN, CUST and SEN. He wanted to answer them quickly and move on. But one question asked how many businesses there are and who sits above them, and he had no idea what it meant. Then it clicked. Each code is just one question, answered with a word and a number. And the number tells the factory which walls the app will need.

These questions are called the 20 codes. A code is a short name for one question about how the app handles businesses, people and data. The full set was frozen on 19 Oct 2026, which means it is fixed and every new idea answers the same 20. They sit in 4 groups. Group 1, Businesses, has 6 codes. Group 2, Who Signs In, has 5. Group 3, Customers, has 4. Group 4, Public, has 5. The groups move from the inside of the app outwards: from the businesses at the core to strangers on the internet.

Every answer is a word plus a number. The word is the code, written in capitals. The number is the level, and each code has its own range. For example, TEN (0-6) counts the layers of businesses. A low number means simple. A high number means more layers, and so more care. The answers are written together, like TEN 3 or SEN 1. Most codes also point to a wall, numbered W1 to W8, that will keep data apart. So by answering the codes, you are already deciding which walls the app will need.

Group 1 is Businesses. TEN (0-6) asks how many businesses use the app and who sits above them. Litterline is TEN 3: breeders, with one reseller, Northvale Canine Society, above them. PARENTS (0-3) asks how many parties one business can sit under. DOWN (0-3) asks what a party above may do to the layers below, such as read them. MEET (0-3) asks whether businesses that do not own each other ever meet. POOL (0-3) asks whether anything is worked out across businesses. EXIT (0-3) asks what happens to a business's data when it leaves.

Group 2 is Who Signs In. ROLE (0-2) asks whether all of a business's staff have the same access, or different roles with different access. BELONG (0-2) asks how many businesses one person can belong to. GUEST (0-1) asks whether a business can let an outside person in with a limited view. HELP (0-3) asks whether someone can see the app as another person sees it, for example to give support, and whether that is logged. JOIN (0-2) asks who can create an account: invite only, people with a join link, or anyone who signs in.

Group 3 is Customers. CUST (0-5) asks how far into a business's own customers the app goes. At 0 only staff use it. At 3, customers can write their own data, and at 5 customers sell to each other. Litterline is CUST 3. PRICE (0-3) asks whether everyone pays the same price. SHARE (0-1) asks whether members can share things with each other. SEN (0-3) asks how sensitive the data is: 0 is ordinary, 1 is personal, 2 is health or clinical, and 3 is children. SEN matters a great deal, as the next lesson shows.

Group 4 is Public. VIEW (0-2) asks whether a stranger can read anything without signing in. POST (0-2) asks whether a stranger can put data in without signing in, such as through a contact form. BRAND (0-2) asks whose name is on the screen: none, one brand, or a brand per business. Litterline is BRAND 2, because each reselling club shows its own name. SEND (0-2) asks what the app sends to outside systems the factory does not control. PAY (0-2) asks whether money moves between parties in one chain, such as payouts within that chain.

Do you need all 20 answers on day one? No. Six codes are required, because the factory cannot judge an idea without them: TEN, CUST, SEN, VIEW, POST and PAY. The other 14 may be answered with a question mark, meaning not yet known. That keeps the form honest. A guess dressed up as a fact is worse than an open question, because nobody goes back to check it. The answers go straight onto the card, which is their only home. Rory filled in the six, marked three others as not yet known, and finished. Let us recap.
