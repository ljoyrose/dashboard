# The Research Sweep
M02 · Stage 1 · Research · Lesson 1 · about 6 min · 23 Oct 2026

**Video slot:** provider `rosequest` · id `TO CONFIRM` · type `v` · key `M02-L1`

**You can:** You can list the parts of the research sweep and explain why customers' own words come before our own ideas.

Esme's neighbour runs Hollin Lane Kennels, a boarding kennel. Esme had heard her talk about inspection day, when an inspector checks the kennel's records, and she wanted to build Stayproof, an inspection pack for kennels. She was sure she knew what kennel owners needed: a smart dashboard with charts. But when she read what kennel owners actually wrote online, almost nobody mentioned charts. They wrote about logs scattered everywhere and panic the night before an inspection. Esme's lesson was clear. Research means listening to customers in their own words before you decide anything.

Once an idea passes Screen, it moves to Stage 1, Research, in the room of the same name. Like Stage 0, research is free: no build credits are spent. The goal is to find out whether real people need this app, what they already use, and what rules apply. The very first job is to check what the factory already owns. Earlier cards, research or mockups for the same idea are searched for by name, including old names, and linked to the card. There is no point researching from scratch what was already found last month.

Next comes the competitor sweep. A competitor, or rival, is any product people already use to solve the same problem. The sweep picks 3 to 5 rivals and records their prices and their reviews. The most useful reviews are the 1 and 2 star ones, because they show exactly what breaks and what annoys people. Every source is dated, so anyone can see how fresh it is. For Stayproof, the rivals might be general kennel software, paper record books and spreadsheets. Even a paper notebook counts as a rival, if that is what owners use today.

The sweep then collects customer words. This means 10 exact phrases people use for the pain, and 10 for the result they want, copied from reviews and groups word for word. Their words, not ours. These phrases are used later for headlines, the sales page and the first screens a new user sees. Alongside them comes review mining: every complaint and feature request is quoted with its link and date, then counted by theme. The top ten themes come first. If forty reviews mention lost logs, that matters more than one clever idea of our own.

With rivals and customer words in hand, we build a gap matrix. A gap matrix is a table. Its rows are features, taken from rivals and from what reviews ask for. Its columns are the rivals. Each box is marked to show one of three things: a feature missing everywhere, a feature customers ask for, or a feature done badly, judged by those 1 and 2 star reviews. The matrix ends with the 3 strongest gaps. Those gaps become our edge, meaning the reason a customer would choose this app instead of what they use now.

Next, the market. Market size means roughly how many possible customers there are, and the trend means whether that number is growing or shrinking. Both must come with sources, never guesses. The audience data must also be lawful. That means using public counts, trade bodies, official company records and search trends, and never scraping people's personal details. Scraping means using a program to copy information off web pages in bulk. A factory that broke data rules while researching an app that must follow data rules would be building on sand.

Then comes the rules flag, short for the regulatory flag. A regulation is a law or official rule that a business must follow. The flag records which ones touch this idea, and whether any of them could stop it altogether. This line is done by a person, not a tool, because judging the law needs a human who understands it. For Stayproof, the flag would name the boarding licence rules, such as the England 2018 Regulations. For Litterline, it would name the dog breeding licence rules. The flag does not decide anything yet. It warns the next gate.

Finally, everything is pulled together into an idea code and a rank. The rank scores four things: market, profit, risk and confidence, which is how sure we are of the evidence. A one-page offer draft is also written, describing the dream customer, the pain in their words and what life looks like after. That page is used again at Stage 7 for the sales page. Esme's research turned a charts app into a logs and inspection app that kennel owners actually asked for. Now the research goes to a person at the Merit gate. First, a recap.

## Recap
- Check What We Already Own First
- Collect Rivals, Reviews And Customers' Exact Words
- Gap Matrix, Market, Rules Flag, Then Rank
- **Why It Matters:** Customers' Own Words Beat Our Own Guesses

## Quiz
1. Which reviews does the competitor sweep look at most closely?
   - A) 5 star reviews
   - B) 4 star reviews
   - C) 3 star reviews
   - D) 1 and 2 star reviews
   - Answer: D. Low-star reviews show what breaks and what annoys people, which is where the gaps are.
2. How many exact customer phrases are collected for the pain?
   - A) 3
   - B) 5
   - C) 10
   - D) 20
   - Answer: C. The sweep collects 10 phrases for the pain and 10 for the result, word for word.
3. What does the gap matrix end with?
   - A) The 3 strongest gaps
   - B) A finished app
   - C) A price list
   - D) A list of test accounts
   - Answer: A. The matrix ends with the 3 strongest gaps, which become the app's edge.
4. Who completes the rules flag?
   - A) An AI tool on its own
   - B) A person, because judging the law needs a human
   - C) The first customer
   - D) Nobody, it is optional
   - Answer: B. The regulatory flag is a person's line, because no tool can judge the law.
