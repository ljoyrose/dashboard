## Slide 1 · Title
**Beat:** Title Hook · 133 words · about 61 s

Welcome, and thank you for being here. In the next twenty minutes or so, I'm going to show you how an app gets made in a factory rather than in a back bedroom. You'll see the road every idea travels, the gates where a real person checks the work, the walls that keep your customers' data apart, and how your business can start with one short form. One thing before we begin: every business, person and number you'll see today is invented. Some of the screens show where the factory is heading by 2029, and those are clearly labelled as a vision, not today's figures. Nothing here is anyone's private data. So sit back, and let's walk through the factory together, from the front door to the radar that watches every live app.

## Slide 2 · The Promise
**Beat:** Promise · 130 words · about 60 s

Here's my promise to you. By the end of this talk, you'll know the nine stages an app goes through, from a one-line idea to a live app with paying customers. You'll know the gates where a person says yes or no, and why those gates save you money. You'll know what the factory needs from you, and it isn't code. And you'll know exactly what you could do on Monday morning to start. You don't need to be technical. You don't need to know what a database is. What you need is to know your customers: who they are, what they pay for, and what keeps them up at night. That part only you can bring, and the factory is built to turn that knowledge into a working app.

## Slide 3 · Who This Is For
**Beat:** Who It's For / Not For · 117 words · about 54 s

So who is this for? It's for owners of service businesses with repeat customers: people who come back every month, every season or every litter. It's especially for businesses where a licence or a rulebook shapes the work, like breeders, boarding kennels, groomers and clinics, because rules are exactly where a well-built app earns its keep. It's for anyone who wants their own name and colours on the screen rather than someone else's logo. And it's honestly not for everyone. If you want a novelty app that people open once and forget, the factory will stop it at the very first gate. That isn't harsh. It's the factory protecting your money before a single credit is spent.

## Slide 4 · Where This Started
**Beat:** Origin Story · Epiphany Bridge: Backstory, Desire, Wall · 126 words · about 58 s

Let me tell you where this started. And a note for whoever presents this: this story is to be checked with Lisa before use. I run animal businesses, and I trained as a solicitor, so rules and records are part of my everyday life. I wanted simple tools for my own clients. Every app I paid for was hand-made from nothing. Each one had different gaps. When I asked a simple question, 'can one customer ever see another customer's records?', nobody could give me a straight answer with proof. And none of those apps learned anything from the one before. That was my wall. I had good ideas, and I had customers who wanted them, but I had fragile apps and no way to trust them.

## Slide 5 · The Moment It Changed
**Beat:** Origin Story · Epiphany Bridge: Epiphany, Plan · 118 words · about 54 s

Then it clicked. Car makers don't hand-carve every car. They have a production line, standard parts and an inspection at every station, and every car that rolls off the line is better because of the ones before it. So I stopped trying to build apps, and I built the factory that builds them. One road with nine stages. A shelf of proven parts that we call bricks. And a gate between every stage, where the work is checked before it's allowed to move on. Each new app reuses what the last one proved. When something goes wrong, the fix becomes a new line on the checklist, so it can't happen again. That's the plan you're about to see.

## Slide 6 · The One Thing
**Beat:** The One Thing · 117 words · about 54 s

If you remember one slide from today, make it this one. A factory with gates is what makes a custom app safe, fast and affordable. Here's why. A problem caught at the idea stage costs a short conversation. The same problem caught while building costs credits and time. Caught after launch, it costs customers, money and trust, and sometimes a call from a regulator. So the factory refuses to move an app forward until the evidence for that stage is there. Every stage has a finished-when line, and every gate checks it. That discipline is what lets one small team ship many apps without cutting corners, and it's what lets you trust the app with your customers.

## Slide 7 · Three Things Most People Get Wrong
**Beat:** 3 Secrets Overview · 115 words · about 53 s

Now, there are three beliefs that stop business owners from getting the app they need, and I want to break each one. The first is about the vehicle: the belief that every app has to be invented from nothing, so it must be slow and expensive. The second is about you: the belief that you need to be technical, or hire someone technical, to get an app made. The third is about the world outside: the belief that safety, privacy and the law are someone else's problem, to be sorted out later. Each of the next sections takes one of those beliefs and shows you what the factory does instead. Let's start with the vehicle.

## Slide 8 · Secret 1 · Eight Shelves Of Bricks
**Beat:** Secret 1 · Vehicle: Teach · 132 words · about 61 s

Secret one. Apps are assembled, not invented. Every app is built from bricks: small, proven parts, each one already tested in other apps. There are one hundred and one bricks in the register today, and they sit on eight shelves. BASICS is the floor that every app gets: sign-in and menus. Boundaries holds the walls between businesses, customers and the public. Bucks handles plans and payments. Body is everything your customers actually see, like bookings and records. Brand swaps the name, colours and logo. Boost gets you found, with public pages and reviews. Brains works things out, like scores and reports. And Bots run on their own, sending reminders and doing imports. Bricks then group into kits, numbered K00 to K08: a booking kit, a client portal kit, a breeding records kit.

## Slide 9 · Secret 1 · One Road, Nine Stages
**Beat:** Secret 1 · Vehicle: Proof · 129 words · about 60 s

And every app travels the same road. Stage zero is your idea, sitting in reception. Stage one is research: competitors, customer words, market size and any rules that apply. Stage two picks a base to start from. Stage three chooses the features, and who sees what. Stage four builds the tables, the structure that holds the data. Stage five sets up test businesses and test people. Stage six writes the build prompts, and this is the only stage that spends paid build credits. Stage seven is launch, with a sales page and your domain. Stage eight is operate, where the app is watched every single day. Between stages sit the gates, and six of them need a person's yes. Notice that nothing is spent until the plan is locked.

## Slide 10 · Secret 2 · You Just Fill In A Form
**Beat:** Secret 2 · Internal: Teach · 134 words · about 62 s

Secret two. You don't need to be technical; you need to be clear. Your part starts with one form. It asks for your vision in a sentence, who pays, and the pain you solve for them. Then it asks twenty short code questions, in plain words. For example: can a stranger read anything without signing in? Can one person belong to two businesses? Six of those questions must be answered. The rest are allowed to say 'not yet known', and the factory will find out. Then come three kill questions: could it give wrong answers that look right, would people use it once and stop, and is there no reason to come back. Finally the factory suggests a verdict, pass, hold or kill, and the data role: who is responsible for the personal data.

## Slide 11 · Secret 2 · 20 Codes In 4 Groups
**Beat:** Secret 2 · Internal: Proof · 124 words · about 57 s

Those twenty answers become a code: a word plus a number for each part. They sit in four groups, from the inside out. Businesses: how many businesses use the app and who sits above them, from TEN zero, no data stored, up to TEN six, several governing bodies. Who signs in: whether staff have different roles, whether guests can be let in, how people join. Customers: how deep the app goes into your customers, from staff-only records up to customers selling to each other, and how sensitive the data is, from ordinary to children's data. And public: what a stranger can see or send without signing in. Together these numbers tell the factory exactly which walls your app needs, before anyone builds a thing.

## Slide 12 · Secret 3 · Walls Keep Data Apart
**Beat:** Secret 3 · External: Teach · 118 words · about 54 s

Secret three. Safety and the law are built in, not bolted on at the end. The factory has eight walls, and each app gets the ones its codes call for. The brand wall means one business can never see another business's data, even inside the same app. The member wall means one customer can never see another customer's records. The public wall controls exactly what a signed-out stranger can read or submit. And the sensitivity hold stops any app that handles health or children's data until a proper data protection impact check has been done and signed. These walls aren't promises in a brochure. As you're about to see, they're tested, again and again, from outside the app.

## Slide 13 · Secret 3 · Tested After Every Paid Step
**Beat:** Secret 3 · External: Proof · 119 words · about 55 s

Here's how they're tested. Every time the builder does paid work, six things happen, in this order, every single time. First we record what was done. Second we check what it cost, and whether anything should be refunded. Third we publish. Fourth we re-probe every table, to make sure nothing has opened up. Fifth we test as real members: an existing member and a brand-new member of each brand do the app's main job from start to finish. Then a member of brand A deliberately tries to read brand B's records, and that test must fail. Sixth, and only then, the step is marked done. If anything leaks at any point, the build stops until it's fixed and proved.

## Slide 14 · The Factory In 2029
**Beat:** Future Pacing · 114 words · about 53 s

Now let me show you where this is heading. These are vision figures for 2029, clearly illustrative, not today's numbers. The target set for the factory is one new app a day, for three years. That adds up to roughly a thousand live apps. Every one of them is probed for leaks every morning at twenty to seven, on every table. Every one is watched on the radar for problems and for money: is it earning more than it costs? And the person running the factory only sees real decisions. The decide queue is capped at ten, so nothing important gets buried under noise. That's what a factory buys you: scale without losing care.

## Slide 15 · Fully Automated, Except The Gates
**Beat:** Future Pacing · Proof · 115 words · about 53 s

And this is the 2029 picture of automation. Every step you see runs by itself: the research sweep, the tables, the test data, the build prompts and the launch pages. The only human touch points are the gates. Screen, where the idea is first judged. Merit, where research says it's worth building. Lock, where the feature list is frozen. Invest, where credits are committed. Send, where the build is proved live. And Launch, where real customers are let in. Those are the moments where judgement, law and money decisions live. A machine can prepare everything for them, but a person should still say yes. That balance is deliberate, and it's what keeps the factory trustworthy.

## Slide 16 · One App, Many Brands
**Beat:** Case Study · 112 words · about 52 s

Here's an invented example of one app in many brands. Litterline is a licence and records hub for dog breeders: litters, health records, kennel logs and the evidence a council inspector asks for. Under the LJR7 brand, it's sold directly. Under the Puppy Classroom jacket, it's exactly the same app with a different name, colours and join link, sold to Puppy Classroom's own audience. And under your brand, it's yours: your name, your colours, your domain. Nothing is rebuilt for each brand. The Brand shelf swaps the jacket, and the walls keep every brand's members completely apart. That's what white-label means in the factory: one app, built once, worn by many brands.

## Slide 17 · Reseller Rule: Never Free
**Beat:** Case Study · Rule · 124 words · about 57 s

One rule matters a lot for resellers. A reseller brand never offers a free plan. Your landing page button carries both your brand and the plan, for example join, slash, ppp, with plan equals plus-yearly. The member signs in first and pays second, so nobody pays without an account. When they come back from sign-in, the server checks that the plan really belongs to your brand, and it refuses a free plan on a reseller brand. It records the chosen plan against the member, even if they abandon checkout, so you can follow up. And the price is never read from the web address, so nobody can edit a link to pay less. Each payment is stamped with the brand that made the sale.

**Beat:** The Stack · 118 words · about 54 s

## Slide 20 · Two Choices
**Beat:** Two Choices · 108 words · about 50 s

So you have two choices. The first is to carry on as you are: paper forms, spreadsheets, and apps that don't talk to each other, with records scattered everywhere when the inspector calls. That choice costs nothing today, but it keeps costing you time every week.
